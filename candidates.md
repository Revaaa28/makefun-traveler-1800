# Candidates — One Sentence for a Traveler from 1800

Competition: u-po-pu-one-sentence-for-a-traveler-from-1800-7e6162fb
Judge brief criteria, in order:
1. Explains the modern internet so a person from 1800 grasps what it is and what it does.
2. Builds from what an 1800 person already knows (road, post office, library) — no words coined after their time.
3. True of the internet as it is now; nothing that would mislead a literal reader.
4. Every word earns its place inside 200 chars; no filler, no crammed list of features.
5. Leaves one image or phrase a reader would still remember a week later.

House rules for this file: target 100–160 chars, at least two beats (setup → turn/reframe),
one concrete detail, punch in the last 3–5 words. No post-1800 words anywhere
(no "internet", "computer", "screen", "wire", "electric", "digital", "online", "phone").

## The candidates

C1. Imagine every letter ever written and every book ever printed gathered in one great room, where any man may read any of them, and send his own word across the sea, in the blink of an eye.

C2. Picture a post office holding every letter ever written, where any man may read any page and send his own word across the sea before the ink dries.

C3. It is a great library holding every book, letter, and song in the world, open to any man at any hour, where a word sent here reaches the far side of the earth before the ink dries.

C4. Imagine a road of light joining every house on earth, so a letter written here reaches a stranger across the sea before the ink is dry.

C5. Every book, letter, and song in the world waits in one great room, and any man may open any of them, and speak to any stranger, whenever he wishes.

C6. Think of a post office with no walls, where every letter ever written waits to be read, and your own word reaches any man on earth before the ink dries.

C7. A single great library holds every letter, book, and song in the world, and any man may read any page, and send his own word across the sea, the moment he asks.

C8. Imagine a market square where every man on earth may speak at once, and every word is heard the moment it is spoken, by anyone who cares to listen.

C9. It is as if every letter ever written, every book ever printed, sat in one room, open to any man at any hour, for nothing.

C10. Picture every letter ever written gathered in one great post house, where any man may read any of them, and his own word crosses the sea before the ink dries.

C11. Imagine a library the size of the world, where any man may read any book ever written, and whisper a word that a stranger across the sea hears at once.

C12. A great road joins every house on earth, carrying every letter ever written, so any man may read any page and speak to any stranger in the blink of an eye.

## Manual ranking (against the brief, in order — no judge API used)

Measured: all 12 are one sentence, 122–187 chars, zero post-1800 words.

- C1 — 187 chars: covers both halves (read + send) but over the 160 target, baggy
  ("every letter ever written and every book ever printed" is list-like), and its
  punch "in the blink of an eye" is a cliché. Loses on criterion 4 and 5.
- C2 — 147 chars: post office holding every letter (knowledge) → any man may read
  any page AND send his own word across the sea (doing) → punch "before the ink
  dries". Agency for the traveler, concrete detail (ink), strongest closing image.
- C3 — 180 chars: covers both halves but over target; "open to any man at any hour"
  plus "far side of the earth" is two place/time phrases doing one job. Loses to C2
  on criterion 4.
- C4 — 135 chars: road of light, only the messaging half — no knowledge access, so
  it loses to C2 on criterion 1 (the internet is also the library, not just the mail).
- C5 — 147 chars: covers both halves, but ends flat on "whenever he wishes" and the
  double "any" clauses read list-like. Loses to C2 on criterion 5.
- C6 — 152 chars: "post office with no walls" is vivid and "any man on earth" is a
  strong sending claim, but "waits to be read" is passive where C2 gives the
  traveler agency ("any man may read any page"), and "Think of" opens weaker than
  "Picture". Loses to C2 by a hair on criteria 1 and 5.
- C7 — 160 chars: covers both halves, but "every letter, book, and song" is a
  three-item list (criterion 4) and it ends on the weak "the moment he asks".
  Loses to C2 on criteria 4 and 5.
- C8 — 147 chars: market square, only the talking half — no knowledge access, so it
  loses on criterion 1 like C4, and "by anyone who cares to listen" is filler.
- C9 — 122 chars: access only, no sending (loses criterion 1), and "for nothing"
  overclaims — access is not literally free (criterion 3 risk).
- C10 — 158 chars: near-twin of C2, but "post house" is less familiar than the
  brief's own "post office" (criterion 2), and it is 11 chars longer for the same
  picture. Loses to C2 on criteria 2 and 4.
- C11 — 151 chars: "library the size of the world" is strong, but "whisper a word
  that a stranger hears at once" is vaguer than C2's "send his own word across the
  sea", and "at once" is a weak punch. Loses to C2 on criteria 1 and 5.
- C12 — 155 chars: road metaphor is more abstract than the post office for a
  traveler (criterion 2), and "in the blink of an eye" is a cliché punch.
  Loses to C2 on criteria 2 and 5.

Pairwise decider, C2 vs C6 (the two closest): both are post-office + "before the ink
dries". C2 wins because the traveler acts ("any man may read any page and send his
own word") while C6's letters merely "wait to be read", and "Picture" opens stronger
than "Think of". C2 also beats C10 (same picture, plainer words, 11 chars shorter).

## WINNER — C2 (147 chars)

Picture a post office holding every letter ever written, where any man may read any page and send his own word across the sea before the ink dries.
